Security in the kernel, not in the ORM

Security in the kernel

RBAC and row policies are compiled into the predicate the executor evaluates and the bounds it scans — not checked by a wrapper a caller could route around.

Why this is structural

This is the part most key-value-layer projects get wrong, and it is a matter of structure rather than of care. Policies are not checked by a wrapper a caller could step around: they are compiled into the predicate the executor evaluates and into the bounds it scans. Every read and write on RecordTransaction takes a SecurityContext, so there is no unauthenticated door — bypassing is possible, but only by naming SecurityContext::superuser(), which is one grep away.

Before any row is touched: an RBAC check against the catalog, a mandatory tenant restriction on tenant-scoped tables, then the OR of the matching row-level-security policies. Writes are checked in both directions as in Postgres — an update may only touch a row the caller can already see and may only leave behind a row they could have written, so a policy cannot be escaped by editing your way out of it.

The head node does not authenticate. It reads the caller's identity from request headers and trusts them. That is deliberate and it is documented on the concepts page — but it means a node reachable by anything other than a proxy you control is a node anyone can be a superuser on.
